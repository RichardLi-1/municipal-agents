The problem

Budgets, transit changes, rezonings and new facilities are explained in long staff reports, often published shortly before the meeting. Affected residents find out late, can't tell how it touches them, and miss the chance to speak. Municipal Agents drafts a plain brief from the city's own documents; code checks every quote is on its page, and a named staff member approves each statement. Lenses add transit access, lifecycle cost, commitments and housing.

Case study: Region of Waterloo 2027 budget

Decision: Regional Council sets the 2027 budget, which fixes regional property tax and water rates. Draft to Council 16 Dec 2026 (register to speak by noon 9 Dec); review days 19 and 27 Jan; EngageWR survey open to 26 Feb 2027. The draft isn't public yet, so the brief uses last year's forecast for 2027, and says so.

Sources: 19 public documents, 344 pages, from the Region, GRT and Kitchener. Two more decisions: water supply strategy, transit hub variances.

First run: US$0.24 for the full brief with four lenses: 17 statements, each quote found on its page, 8 figures, 6 unknowns. Reading grade 11.8, target 8 TODO(verify) after rewrite. Not yet staff-reviewed.

Lesson: the timeline took most care; two official pages disagree on a review-day date.

Data and resources needed

  • Reports, agendas, budgets, plans as text PDFs; per decision, a clerk-checked timeline and map area.
  • GTFS, OpenStreetMap, permits, census households; a trained reviewer; a capped model API key.

How another municipality adapts it

  1. cities/<id>.json: map, languages, clerk, GTFS. ~1 h
  2. PDFs into data/sources; npm run sources. ~1 h
  3. data/decisions/<id>.json, clerk-checked. ~2–4 h each
  4. Local GTFS, OpenStreetMap, permits (5 stages). ~1–3 days
  5. Model and spend caps; deploy the container. ~½ day
  6. Train reviewers. ~1 h

Times are estimates. Text PDFs and public GTFS need no code changes.

Who would use it

  • Residents, no account: renters, owners, riders, seniors, students, parents, people with a disability, newcomers.
  • Clerks, communications: review each statement, publish.
  • Planners: transit, cost, housing lenses from open data.
  • Councillors, groups: one cited link with the dates to take part.

Privacy and the law

  • No personal data by design. No accounts; only public documents go to the model. IP addresses stay in memory for rate limits, never logged; a logged question keeps only its text, audiences and language. Questions are public: we ask people not to type personal details.
  • MFIPPA. A municipality may collect personal information only if a statute authorizes it, for law enforcement, or if necessary to a lawfully authorized activity, with notice (ss. 28(2), 29(2)).4 Still do a privacy impact assessment.
  • Data residency. The Gemini API is a US service; paid-tier content is not used to improve Google's products.5 For production: Vertex AI in Canada, which lists Gemini 3.5 and 3.8 Flash but not 3.6,6 or an on-premises model.
  • Bill 194. Ontario's Enhancing Digital Security and Trust Act, 2024 lets the province require prescribed public sector entities that use AI (municipalities can be prescribed) to inform the public, keep an accountability framework, manage risk and have a person oversee it.1,2 No regulation yet does so for municipal AI.3 The prototype already discloses AI use and names a human approver.

What residents should know

  • AI drafts; a named staff member approves each statement.
  • Not the official notice: each page links to it, and it prevails.
  • Every number links to its page. The AI can be wrong.
  • Questions are public, not tied to you. Translations are by machine TODO(verify).
  • Errors: "Report an error" or the clerk TODO(verify).

What's next

Pilot one live decision with a Waterloo Region municipality; measure review time and understanding with screen-reader users and newcomers. Before real use: WCAG 2.2 AA audit, privacy impact assessment, Canadian-hosted model, OCR, clerk sign-off procedure.
